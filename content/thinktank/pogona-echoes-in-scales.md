On a summer evening in 1949, deep in the Syrian interior, archaeologists unearthed an ancient coin. Weighing just 7.7 grams, the alloy piece depicted the Hellenistic ruler Seleucus II Callinicus Pogon. Despite his epithet Callinicus, meaning "gloriously triumphant", his reign over the Seleucid Empire was rather defined by fragmentation and turmoil. Yet, this was not his only onomastic contradiction. Judging by the smooth-faced profile stamped onto the metal, he seemingly possessed one of the most cleanly shaven faces in ancient history, even though he bore the nickname *Pogon*, meaning "the bearded." Given that ancient Greek philosophy was among the first to formally consider the ethical standing of animals, it is a charming irony that his moniker eventually inspired the scientific name of a remarkable modern species: the bearded dragon.

![Coin depicting Seleucus II Callinicus Pogon, unearthed in the Syrian interior in 1949.](assets/seleucus-pogon-coin.jpg)

When the prolific Australian herpetologist Glen Milton Storr classified hundreds of reptile species, he likely didn't struggle to name the genus to which the bearded dragon belongs: *Pogona*. Binomial nomenclature has always invited a touch of taxonomic creativity. Across millions of years of evolution, the dragon's formidable armor—hard, spiny scales that appear razor-sharp to the touch—has served as a potent deterrent against hungry goannas, dingoes, and birds of prey.

## Survival in the Unforgiving Desert

The adaptive brilliance of organisms remains a marvel of the natural world. Much as human survival has relied on the evolution of our complex brains, bearded dragons have engineered their own physical and behavioral strategies to endure the unforgiving desert. Deep in the subtropical woodlands and savannas of Australia, they dutifully patrol their territories, claiming prime basking spots to soak up the sun for large parts of the day. In doing so, they participate in an ancient, high-stakes game of dominance, securing another day of life in the silent hope that their offspring might one day inherit the same instinct to survive.

## The Black Box of Consciousness

Studying living beings often requires a degree of scientific detachment. Much of what we know about the creatures we share this planet with did not emerge from personal acquaintance, but from observing them as predictable subjects, organisms defined by quantifiable behaviours. Yet, reducing animals to mere data points risks doing a profound injustice to the beauty of existence and the vast, mysterious spectrum of perception. For us, smelling a flower is rarely just the mechanical activation of olfactory pathways; it is a subjective experience. Similarly, the internal world of an animal is constructed from its own unique reality. Their inner experiences remain a "black box" just as inexplicable as our own, something we cannot ever fully decipher.

Can we ever then truly step into the consciousness of another creature? Can we feel what they feel and experience what they experience at any given moment? Or are we forever isolated by the membrane of our own minds, bound to a world that exists only as it appears to us?
