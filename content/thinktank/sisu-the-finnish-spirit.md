It was a cold January day as the sun began to show its face over the horizon in Ladoga's Karelia. Knowing that the daylight would only last five hours, *allikersantti* Simo stretches his snow-white camouflage over his head hastily. He has already prepared for the coming minutes, having packed mounds of snow in front of him to conceal his muzzle blast. Knowing that a Russian squad will pass by any moment, he pushes a handful of snow into his mouth. There is no movement around him, not even the steam of a warm breath. The only contrast of color in a sea of white is the metallic gray on his SAKO rifle, nicknamed *Pystykorva* ("The Spitz"). Simo aligns his eye through the spitz-shaped iron sight and locks onto a young *ryadovoy*. His gray uniform, obtrusive as it may seem, sticks out from the endless white horizon. In an instant, the deafening screech of the 7.62mm awakens the dead forest from its sleep.

## The Legend of the White Death

Simo Häyhä, also known as "the White Death" by the Russians, was a Finnish hero of mythical proportions during the Winter War of 1939–1940. He was a farmer and hunter prior to his military service, living a serene rural life. Maybe it was back then when he developed his sixth sense for the environment the war would be fought on, and the rifle he fought with. Over the course of the Winter War, he would average five body counts a day spread over 100 days, making him one of the deadliest snipers in major warfare.

After the war, he would usually refuse to comment on his wartime actions. His actions were met with either admiration of wartime heroism or hatred for the cold and systematic way he conducted his duties. When asked if he felt remorse, he replied:

> "I did what I was told to do, as well as I could. There would be no Finland unless everyone else had done the same."

## The Crucible of the Winter War

This sentence could summarize the wartime effort of Finland during the Winter War. The Kremlin, fearing a potential Nazi offensive through Finland, had called for a land swap that would push the Finnish border away from Leningrad (now St. Petersburg). Finland declined and as a result, on November 30, 1939, nearly half a million Soviet troops stormed into Finland.

The Soviets imagined that their staggering superiority in numbers and resources would be enough to quickly overwhelm the undermanned and lightly armed Finns. Men and arms were poured into Finland but were almost immediately bogged down due to the Finns' fierce resistance and guerrilla tactics. A much-anticipated victory for the Soviets was served as a cold dish, tasting bitter, with 65,000 killed and wounded on the Finnish side and anywhere between 300,000 to one million casualties on the Russian side.

## The Discovery of Sisu

Finnish perseverance in the face of invasion was described by *Time* magazine in January 1940 with a very apt term: **Sisu**.

> "The Finns have something they call sisu. It is a compound of bravado and bravery, of ferocity and tenacity, of the ability to keep fighting after most people would have quit, and to fight with the will to win. The Finns translate sisu as 'the Finnish spirit' but it is a much more gutful word than that. Last week the Finns gave the world a good example of sisu by carrying the war into Russian territory on one front while on another they withstood merciless attacks by a reinforced Russian Army. In the frozen wilderness that forms most of the Russo-Finnish frontier between Lake Laatokka and the Arctic Ocean, the Finns definitely gained the upper hand."

Sisu is a concept that is thought to not have a literal equivalent in other languages. No wonder then, this Finnish concept is held by Finns themselves proudly to express their national character. Sisu can be described in many ways—stoic determination, grit, or hardiness. However, none of these terms could accurately circumscribe the full concept of sisu.

Sisu is best described as extraordinary courage and determination in the face of adversity. Even though this concept is materialized as a word in Finland, it is a universal human trait relevant to people all over the world. At the core of sisu, there is more strength in each of us than what appears on the surface. Sisu is the embodiment of defying the odds and cultivating hope when there doesn't seem to be any.

## A Cognitive Muscle and Psychological Potential

William R. Aho, professor emeritus of sociology at Rhodes College, expressed his opinion on the lack of research on this concept, stating:

> "We need a good deal of organized, systematic scientific research to discover the scope and depth of sisu, geographically and situationally, and the depth and strength of both the beliefs and behaviors surrounding and emanating from sisu."

Thus, attempts were made by researchers attempting to shed light on this concept in a scientific manner. Various paths of research led to the introduction of sisu as psychological power potential during the 3rd World Congress on Positive Psychology in Los Angeles in 2013[^1]. In this study, sisu is seen as a *"psychological competence that enables extraordinary action to overcome a mentally or physically challenging situation"*.

More interestingly, sisu is described as something that contributes to what is called the **action mindset**, a consistent approach toward challenges that at first seem to exceed our capacities. So, with that, it exceeds the boundaries of being only a cultural abstraction and becomes a cognitive muscle that can be trained and strengthened. 

## Cultivating Sisu in Everyday Life

Fortunately, most who read this piece will be living in a time of peace, privileged with the luxuries that modern life can provide, in a country that is stable and prosperous. We might not come close to experiencing a world war - which is by no means a bad thing.

Luckily, people like you and I can do things in our daily lives to nurture a spirit of sisu. With patient practice, it is possible to cultivate the sisu mindset, one that is open to new things and is willing to act. The obstacles we face every day, however small they might be, present us with opportunities to do so.

It is done through making a phone call you have been putting off, speaking up during a meeting, or making that difficult appointment you are not looking forward to. Things that usually give us a sense of anxiety, fear, or any negative emotion are chances for us to put our confidence and determination into action.

Next time you carry through with a difficult task, pay close attention to the feeling of relief, the feeling of accomplishment that follows afterwards. Make peace with it, remember it, maybe even cherish it. With that, you will become more aware of the growth you experience from hardship and thrive as a result. You will be surprised to see how much sisu you already possess within you.
