In the high, wind-swept steppes of Central Anatolia, the physical landscape is famously austere. I have many memories as a child walking through these barren hills. Yet out of this dry earth emerged, in my perspective, one of the world's most radically compassionate ethical traditions: radical benevolence.
![The wind-swept steppes of Central Anatolia.](assets/anatolian-steppe.jpg)

In Anatolian folk poetry and music, carried by roving *aşıks* (troubadours), and the musicians of my hometown Kırşehir, benevolence (*ihsan* and *merhamet*) could be perhaps be considered the ultimate antidote to human cruelty. It is built on a simple premise, to retaliate against malice is to let malice govern your soul.

## The Roots: Gönül and the Refusal to Harm

To understand Central Anatolian benevolence, one must understand the untranslatable concept of *Gönül* (the spiritual heart). In Anatolian thought, the *gönül* is not merely the seat of emotions, but also the sanctuary of the Divine (the House of God). Because God resides in every human heart according to this philosophy, injuring another person's dignity or causing them grief is considered the greatest possible transgression, worse than destroying a physical temple. This is the foundation of an ethos where ethical perfection means refusing to transmit pain, no matter how much pain you receive yourself.

Aşık Veysel, blinded by smallpox in childhood, wrote of the earth and humanity with a radical lack of grievance, treating even betrayal as an invitation to deeper patience: 

> *Bana taşa tutan dostun elinden*
> *Güller topladım da demet eyledim*
> *Kötülük edene iyilik diledim*
> *Ben kendi kendimi kınadım durdum.*


> *From the hand of the friend who pelted me with stones*
> *I gathered roses and made a bouquet*
> *For the one who did me evil, I wished only good*
> *While I stood and blamed only my own self.*

![Aşık Veysel, legendary troubadour of the Anatolian steppe.](assets/asik-veysel.jpg#small)

In Central Anatolia, poetry and philosophy were never locked away in scholastic books. This sacred reverence for the heart was broadcast across the steppe through the wood and wire of the *bağlama* (*saz*). Whether in the satirical verses of Seyrani or the tender *bozlak* laments of modern masters like Neşet Ertaş, music was a ritual of unburdening and teaching that the highest courage is remaining soft in a world that demands you harden.

![My earliest memories are from driving through Anatolian steppes with my grandfather in his Renault 12, listening to saz music. Picture from a movie (we didn't really have cameras back then!)](assets/renault-steppe.jpg)

There are parallels to be drawn to other cultures. Think of the Christian Kenosis (self-emptying love) from the classical Greek philosophy and Christian theology, which states unconditional, sacrificial love that asks for nothing in return and is extended equally to friend and enemy. Or Ubuntu (Southern African humanism) which is often translated as "I am because we are" (Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu - "a person is a person through other persons"). It asserts that human dignity is communal; to dehumanize or harm someone else is to automatically strip yourself of your own humanity. Satyagraha (Gandhian "Truth-Force") is another one, often mistaken for passive resistance, but Gandhi defined it as active, militant love. Its premise is that you defeat an unjust system not by inflicting suffering on the oppressor, but by inviting suffering upon yourself until the oppressor's conscience breaks. Gandhi believed that unretaliated suffering has an alchemical power to convert the oppressor’s moral compass, much like Âşık Paşa wishing sugar for the one who served him poison.

## The Shadow Side: Sanctified Self-Erasure?

Yet when I look beneath the surface, I find myself looking at a troubling philosophical tension. Radical benevolence might be intoxicating in verse, but in practice, where is the line between spiritual mastery and moral self-erasure? Can absorbing repeated cruelty without protest become a form of indifference to injustice committed against oneself? Does rewarding malice with blessings relieve the aggressor of accountability, implicitly validating oppression? When the ego is completely dissolved, does the individual lose the boundary necessary to protect self-worth?

There is a quiet, haunting doubt that shadows the Anatolian ideal: can one be so merciful to the aggressor that one becomes cruel to oneself? I am reminded of Nietzsche's famous critique of "slave morality", arguing that turning the other cheek can be a psychological coping mechanism for the powerless that disguises submission as virtue.

When poets and folk singers practiced radical non-retaliation while living at the absolute bottom of the social hierarchy (landless, poor, and often exploited), their refusal to strike back was both a spiritual halo and a terrestrial cage at the same time. By elevating endurance into almost a holy duty, the culture risked treating the suffering of the gentle as an inevitable, aesthetic virtue. To forgive everything can sometimes mean demanding nothing for your own humanity.

## The Bittersweet Victory of the Garip

This tension is precisely why Central Anatolian folk music is drenched in *hüzün*: a heavy, resonant sorrow. The great *aşıks* did not sing triumphal hymns, they sang laments. I think that they intuitively knew the moral cost of their philosophy, and they accepted it.

To live by radical benevolence is to accept a bittersweet bargain - you preserve the purity of your own *gönül*, but you inherit the bruises of the world. The Anatolian troubadours did not conquer their oppressors, nor did they rid the steppe of cruelty. Instead, they made a conscious, tragic choice, as they chose to break rather than be the ones who break others.

In the end, their legacy is a profound, melancholic monument to human nature, and a subtle reminder that while refusing to echo cruelty may leave your hands empty and your heart scarred, it might be one of the ways to walk through a bitter world without becoming bitter yourself.
